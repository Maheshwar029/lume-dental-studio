Child dental care: prevention, first visits & common questions.

Children's dental care is about prevention, healthy development and building a comfortable relationship with dental visits. Parents play an important role in brushing, diet and recognising problems early.

Patient educationThis guide explains common dental concerns in general terms. It is not a diagnosis and does not replace an examination by a qualified dental professional.

Why does children's dental care matter?

Primary teeth help children eat, speak and maintain space while the mouth develops. Tooth decay can cause pain and infection, so prevention and timely treatment matter even when baby teeth will eventually be replaced.

When should a child first see a dentist?

The American Academy of Pediatric Dentistry recommends establishing a dental home within six months of the first tooth appearing and no later than 12 months of age. Earlier guidance allows the team to assess risk and coach parents on prevention.

What to noticeChanges in pain, swelling, bleeding, sensitivity, function or appearance.
What an assessment addsClinical examination helps separate similar-looking symptoms and identify the right next step.

How can parents reduce the risk of tooth decay?

Parents can help with age-appropriate brushing using fluoride toothpaste, support healthy eating and drinking habits, and keep regular dental appointments. The exact fluoride and brushing advice should be tailored to the child's age and circumstances.

What can a dentist do for a child?

A child-focused dental visit can include examination of teeth and gums, risk assessment, preventive guidance and treatment when needed. The clinician can also help a nervous child become familiar with the dental environment and explain procedures in age-appropriate language.

What if my child is afraid of the dentist?

Tell the dental team before the appointment. A calm introduction, simple explanations, breaks and gradual familiarisation can help. Parents should avoid using frightening language or promising that treatment will never hurt.

When should a child be seen quickly?

Seek prompt dental assessment for significant tooth pain, facial swelling, dental injury, persistent bleeding, a broken tooth or other symptoms that are worsening. A knocked-out permanent tooth is a dental emergency and should be addressed as soon as possible.

Do not delay urgent care

Severe swelling, uncontrolled bleeding, major facial injury or difficulty breathing or swallowing requires urgent medical attention.

Questions patients often ask

When should my child have the first dental visit?

The AAPD recommends a dental home within six months of the first tooth appearing and no later than the first birthday.

Should parents brush children's teeth?

Young children generally need active parental help or supervision because they cannot reliably clean every surface themselves. Your dentist can demonstrate age-appropriate brushing.

What if a baby tooth has a cavity?

Do not assume it can simply be ignored. Decay can cause pain or infection, and the dentist can recommend preventive or restorative care based on the child's needs.
