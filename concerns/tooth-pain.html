Tooth pain: what it can mean and what to do next.

Tooth pain can come from decay, a cracked tooth, an infection, gum problems, sensitivity or other dental conditions. The important step is finding the cause rather than simply masking the symptom.

What does tooth pain feel like?

Tooth pain may be a sharp sensation when you eat or drink, a dull ache, pain when biting, sensitivity to hot or cold, or pain that seems to spread into the jaw or face. The pattern can give a dentist useful clues, but symptoms alone cannot identify the cause.

Common causes of tooth pain

Tooth decay can cause sensitivity and pain as it progresses. Other possible causes include a cracked or damaged tooth, a loose or broken filling, a dental abscess, gum disease, exposed tooth surfaces, grinding, or problems around an erupting wisdom tooth. Sometimes pain is referred from another tooth or area.

Possible sourceDental decay, gum inflammation, sensitivity, damage or infection.
Why assessment mattersDifferent causes can feel similar but need different treatment.

What can a dentist do?

A dentist will ask when the pain started, what triggers it and whether it is constant or intermittent. They examine the tooth, gums and surrounding tissues and may use tests or dental X-rays when appropriate. Treatment depends on the cause and may include a filling, treatment for the nerve inside the tooth, gum treatment, repair of a damaged tooth, or removal when a tooth cannot be predictably saved.

What can you do while waiting?

Keep the area clean and avoid chewing on the painful side. Soft foods and avoiding very hot, cold or sugary foods may make symptoms easier to manage. A pharmacist or dentist can advise whether an appropriate over-the-counter pain reliever is suitable for you. Do not place aspirin directly on a tooth or gum, and do not use leftover antibiotics without professional advice.

When is tooth pain urgent?

Arrange dental care promptly if pain is persistent, worsening, associated with swelling, fever, a bad taste, pain when biting, or does not settle with ordinary pain relief. Facial or neck swelling that affects breathing, swallowing or speaking is an emergency and needs immediate medical attention.

Seek urgent help

If swelling around the mouth, face or neck makes it difficult to breathe, swallow or speak, seek emergency medical care immediately.

How can tooth pain be prevented?

Regular dental examinations, brushing twice a day with fluoride toothpaste, cleaning between teeth daily, limiting frequent sugary foods and drinks, and addressing dental problems early can reduce the risk of painful complications.

Questions patients often ask

Can a toothache go away on its own?

Pain can temporarily settle even when the underlying problem remains. A dentist can determine whether the cause needs treatment.

Does tooth pain always mean I need a root canal?

No. Root canal treatment is only one possible treatment. The appropriate option depends on the tooth, the diagnosis and how much healthy tooth structure remains.

Should I take antibiotics for tooth pain?

Antibiotics are not a substitute for definitive dental treatment and are not appropriate for every toothache. A dentist should assess the cause and whether antibiotics are indicated.

Where to go next

If your symptoms are new, persistent or worrying you, an assessment is the safest way to understand the cause.
